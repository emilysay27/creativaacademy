What Is My Child Learning When They're "Just Playing"?

By the Creativa Academy Team · November 15, 2026 · 5 min read

"What did you do at school today?" — "We just played."

It's one of the most common things parents hear. And it can be unsettling when you're wondering whether your child is actually learning anything, or whether the school is challenging them enough.

Here's the honest answer: at Creativa Academy, play is not a break from learning. It is the learning. And the research on early childhood development is unambiguous on this point. Here's what's actually happening during the activities that look like "just playing."

Block Building

When a child stacks blocks, they're not wasting time. They're developing spatial reasoning — an understanding of how objects relate in three dimensions. They're testing hypotheses ("If I put this big block on top of this small one, will it fall?"). They're building persistence when the tower collapses. And when they build alongside another child, they're negotiating space, sharing materials, and communicating intent.

Block play is one of the most research-supported activities in early childhood. Children who engage in extended block play show stronger math skills in elementary school, particularly in geometry and spatial reasoning. The American Academy of Pediatrics has explicitly recommended open-ended play with blocks as a foundational learning experience.

What you might see at Creativa: A group of 3-year-olds spent three days building a "city" in the block area after noticing a photo book of Miami skylines. Teachers added maps, photos, and conversations about neighborhoods. What looked like play was an extended project in spatial thinking, language, community, and collaboration.

Pretend Play and Dramatic Play

When children play "house," "school," "doctor," or any other pretend scenario, they are doing some of the most cognitively demanding work of early childhood. They are holding an imaginary world in their minds, assigning roles, maintaining those roles, and updating the scenario as it changes — all simultaneously.

This is called symbolic thinking, and it's a fundamental cognitive skill that underlies reading (a letter is a symbol for a sound), math (a number is a symbol for a quantity), and later abstract reasoning. Children who engage in rich pretend play develop stronger executive function — the ability to plan, regulate impulses, and shift attention — than those with limited pretend play experience.

Social skills built during dramatic play are equally significant. Negotiating roles ("I'll be the mom, you be the baby"), managing conflicts ("You said I could have that!"), reading others' emotional states, and recovering from disruptions — these are skills that matter throughout life.

What you might see at Creativa: A "restaurant" in the dramatic play corner, complete with menus the children drew, an ordering system they invented, and a full cast of customers and cooks. Teachers added writing materials, a cash register, and Spanish vocabulary cards. Literacy, math, language, and social skills all woven in.

Art and Making

When a child paints, sculpts with clay, or constructs something with cardboard and tape, they're developing fine motor control — the small precise movements that will eventually make writing possible. They're also developing representation: the understanding that a drawing can stand for something real, and that you can communicate an idea through a mark.

In the Reggio Emilia approach, the visual arts are one of the "hundred languages" through which children think and express ideas. At Creativa, art isn't a Friday afternoon activity; it's a daily medium for exploring concepts across the curriculum.

What you might see at Creativa: After a week exploring the life cycle of butterflies, children painted what they imagined a caterpillar experiences inside a chrysalis. The paintings weren't assessed or graded — they were discussed. What do you see in your painting? What does it feel like in there? The conversation revealed sophisticated thinking about transformation that worksheets would never have surfaced.

Outdoor Play and Movement

Running, climbing, jumping, and playing in sand are not extras that happen when the "real" learning is done. Physical play develops gross motor skills, coordination, and proprioception (the sense of where your body is in space). It builds cardiovascular health and reduces stress hormones that interfere with learning.

Outdoor play also develops risk assessment. A child who climbs and sometimes falls is building the ability to evaluate challenges, manage small consequences, and persist. These are not skills that develop from worksheets.

Research from the CDC and the American Academy of Pediatrics is clear: children who have more physical activity time — including unstructured outdoor play — show better attention and academic performance, not worse.

What This Means for How We Teach

At Creativa, teachers don't just facilitate play and step back. They observe, document, and respond. They ask questions that extend thinking: "What would happen if you added more water to the sand?" They introduce materials that push an inquiry further. They connect what children are exploring through play to language, to drawing, to books, to real-world experiences.

When your child comes home and says "we just played," they're describing their whole day. And the learning — in thinking, language, social skills, creativity, and self-regulation — is woven through all of it.
