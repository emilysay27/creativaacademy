Is My Child's Development on Track? Milestones and When to Ask for Support

By the Creativa Academy Team · November 15, 2026 · 8 min read

Every parent wonders. You watch your child at the park, or drop them off at preschool, and something in you keeps track: Are they doing what other kids their age are doing? Should they be talking more? Playing differently?

These questions are worth taking seriously — and the most important thing to know is that developmental milestones are tools for noticing, not diagnoses. This guide walks through what many children are doing at each age and what to bring to your pediatrician if you have a concern.

Important note: The milestones below describe what many children can do by a given age — not what every child must do. Children develop at different rates. Missing a milestone is a reason to talk with your pediatrician; it is not a diagnosis. Only a qualified professional can evaluate a child's development.

15–17 Months

Communication: Uses at least a few words with meaning (not just babbling). Points to things they want, or to show you something interesting. Understands simple directions like "come here" or "give me the ball."

Play: Begins to pretend — feeding a doll, pretending a block is a car. Explores cause-and-effect toys enthusiastically (push a button, something happens).

Social: Shows clear attachment to familiar people. May be cautious around strangers. Looks to familiar caregivers for reassurance.

Movement: Walking, climbing stairs with help. Begins to run, though falls are still frequent.

Worth mentioning to your doctor if: No words at 15 months; not pointing or waving; lost skills they previously had.

18–23 Months

Communication: At 18 months, most children have around 10–25 words. By 2 years, many are combining two words ("more milk," "daddy go"). Vocabulary grows quickly during this stretch.

Play: Parallel play is normal at this age — playing alongside other children but not yet with them. Begins to imitate household activities (sweeping, talking on the phone).

Social: May become upset when a familiar routine changes. Can show a range of emotions — joy, frustration, affection — and is beginning to notice others' emotions too.

Everyday independence: Interested in feeding themselves with a spoon. Beginning to show preferences in food, clothing, and activities.

Worth mentioning to your doctor if: Fewer than 10 words at 18 months; not combining words at 24 months; loss of previously used language; limited eye contact or social engagement.

2 Years

Communication: Two-word phrases are typical. Many 2-year-olds are understood by familiar people about half the time, and by unfamiliar people somewhat less. Following two-step directions ("Get your shoes and bring them here") is a good sign.

Play: Simple pretend play flourishes — feeding stuffed animals, "cooking" in a play kitchen. Beginning to include others in play, though sharing is still a work in progress.

Social: Big emotions are normal at 2. Children this age are developing self-regulation and need patient, consistent support from adults. Tantrums are typical and are not a sign of a developmental problem.

30 Months (2.5 Years)

Communication: Most 30-month-olds use 3- to 4-word sentences and can be understood by familiar people most of the time. Beginning to ask questions ("What's that?" "Why?").

Play: Pretend play becomes more elaborate — sequences of events, role-play, assigning roles to other people or toys.

Social: More interest in playing with peers. Parallel play continues, but collaborative play emerges.

Worth mentioning to your doctor if: Speech is frequently unclear even to familiar people; child is not asking questions; limited interest in other children.

3 Years

Communication: 3-year-olds typically speak in full sentences and can hold a simple conversation. Strangers can understand them most of the time. They ask many questions and can listen to short stories.

Play: Pretend play is rich and imaginative. Beginning to play cooperatively with peers, taking turns and negotiating roles.

Everyday independence: Dresses with some help, uses the toilet with reminders, feeds themselves reliably.

4 Years

Communication: Uses 4–6 word sentences. Tells stories about things that happened. Strangers understand them without difficulty. Beginning to understand that words can have more than one meaning.

Play: Complex imaginative play — plays "house," creates scenarios, negotiates rules with other children. Understands the concept of taking turns in games.

Social: Friendships become meaningful. Children this age notice when a friend is absent or upset.

5 Years

Communication: Uses complex sentences and varied vocabulary. Can carry on a full conversation. Tells detailed stories with a beginning, middle, and end. Beginning to understand that written symbols represent sounds and words.

Play: Rules-based games with others. Can delay gratification, negotiate, and compromise. Understands fairness.

Everyday independence: Gets dressed independently, manages bathroom routines, helps with simple household tasks.

What to Do If You Have a Concern

If something you've read here resonates — if you've noticed something that doesn't quite match — the right first step is a conversation with your child's pediatrician. You don't need to wait for the next well visit. You can call and say: "I'd like to talk about my child's development and whether a screening makes sense."

Pediatricians use standardized developmental screening tools at routine visits. If a screening suggests further evaluation, your doctor will refer you to specialists — speech-language pathologists, occupational therapists, developmental pediatricians, or other providers depending on the concern.

Early support, when it's needed, works. The earlier a child receives intervention, the more effective it tends to be. Raising a concern is never an overreaction.

Where to Get Help in Florida

Children under 3: Florida Early Steps provides free evaluation and early intervention services. Families can self-refer at floridaearlyinterventionprogram.com or call 1-800-654-4440.

Children 3 and older: Miami-Dade County Public Schools' Child Find program provides free developmental evaluations for preschool-age children. Contact your local elementary school or call Miami-Dade County Public Schools to request a Child Find evaluation.

At Creativa: Our teachers observe children throughout the day and document their development. If a teacher notices something, they will contact you to discuss their observations — not to alarm you, but to share what they're seeing and work together with your family.

Questions About Your Child's Development?

Our teachers observe children closely every day. During your tour, you can ask us anything — about readiness, what we look for, and how we support families when concerns come up.
