My Toddler Isn't Talking Much. What Should I Do?

By the Creativa Academy Team · November 15, 2026 · 5 min read

It starts as a quiet worry. Other children the same age seem to be talking more. Someone mentions that their child is "already saying full sentences." You find yourself counting your toddler's words and wondering if the number is enough.

If this sounds familiar, you're not alone — and you're asking exactly the right question. Here's what communication development looks like in the toddler years, what's worth noting, and how to get the support you need if something seems off.

What Communication Looks Like in Toddlers

Language development covers more than words. Before words come, children communicate through gestures, eye contact, facial expression, and vocalization. These early communication skills are actually strong predictors of language development.

Some checkpoints speech-language pathologists look for:

  • By 12 months: Points to things, waves, looks where you point, responds to their name
  • By 15 months: Uses at least a few words with meaning; points to show you things (not just to get things)
  • By 18 months: Uses approximately 10–25 words; understands simple directions
  • By 24 months: Combines two words ("more water," "daddy go"); vocabulary around 50 or more words; understood by familiar adults at least half the time
  • By 30 months: Uses short sentences of 3–4 words; asks simple questions; understood by unfamiliar people most of the time

These are general ranges based on published guidance from organizations like the American Speech-Language-Hearing Association (ASHA) and the CDC. Children develop at different rates. The ranges don't diagnose — they help you notice.

What About Bilingual Children?

This question comes up constantly at Creativa, and it matters for Miami families in particular. Many children in our community grow up hearing and speaking both English and Spanish at home.

The honest answer from the research: bilingual children are not slower to talk. They may distribute their vocabulary across two languages, so their total word count in one language alone may appear smaller — but their combined vocabulary across both languages is typically comparable to monolingual peers. What speech-language pathologists look for in bilingual children is total communication across all languages, not just one.

The signs that prompt concern are the same regardless of language background: limited eye contact or social engagement, not responding to their name, losing words they previously used, or having a very small total vocabulary across both languages combined.

If you have a concern about a bilingual child, seek out a speech-language pathologist who has experience with bilingual language development — they exist in Miami-Dade, and they can evaluate your child in both languages.

What Observations to Bring to the Pediatrician

When you call or go in to talk with your child's doctor, specific observations are more useful than general worries. Try to bring:

  • Approximately how many words your child uses regularly (even a rough count helps)
  • Whether they combine words, and if so, examples
  • How well strangers can understand them vs. familiar people
  • Whether they point, make eye contact, follow your gaze
  • Whether they've lost any words or communication skills they previously had
  • What languages they hear at home and what they attempt to say in each

A video of your child talking or playing at home can be genuinely useful — many pediatricians welcome it, and it captures the child in a natural setting rather than the potentially stressful exam room.

What Happens After a Concern Is Raised

If your pediatrician shares your concern, they'll likely refer you for a speech-language evaluation. For children under 3 in Florida, this is often done through Florida Early Steps, which provides free early intervention services. For children 3 and older, Miami-Dade County Public Schools' Child Find program provides free evaluations.

An evaluation is not a verdict. It's information — information that tells you whether your child needs support, what kind, and how much. Many children who receive early speech therapy go on to communicate typically and don't need ongoing services. Others benefit from longer-term support.

The one thing that consistently holds across research: earlier support leads to better outcomes. Waiting to see if a child will "catch up on their own" is sometimes the right call, but it's a call best made with a professional, not by waiting alone.

What We See at Creativa

Our teachers observe children's communication throughout the day — during stories, circle time, play, and transitions. We document these observations and share them with families. We're trained to notice, but we don't evaluate. If something catches our attention, we'll bring it to you as a conversation, not a conclusion.

Several families at Creativa have connected with services through our observations. In every case, the family's own instincts and their child's doctor were the ones who made the decisions. We just added another set of eyes.

Where to Get Help in Miami-Dade

Under 3 — Florida Early Steps: Free evaluation and early intervention for children under 3 with developmental delays. Families can self-refer. Visit floridaearlyinterventionprogram.com or call 1-800-654-4440.

Ages 3–5 — Child Find (Miami-Dade County Public Schools): Free developmental evaluations for preschool-age children. Contact Miami-Dade County Public Schools to request a Child Find evaluation through your local elementary school.
