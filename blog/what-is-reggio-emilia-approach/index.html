What Is the Reggio Emilia Approach? A Parent's Guide

By the Creativa Academy Team · November 15, 2026 · 5 min read

When you're researching preschools in Miami, you'll see "Reggio Emilia inspired" on a lot of websites. But what does it actually mean? And more importantly — what will your child's day look like?

Here's the honest answer, without any jargon.

Where Reggio Emilia Comes From

After World War II, the town of Reggio Emilia in northern Italy rebuilt its community schools from the ground up. Parents, teachers, and educators worked together and developed a philosophy centered on one idea: children are capable, curious, and want to learn.

An educator named Loris Malaguzzi led the movement and shaped the approach into what it is today. His most famous idea was that children have "a hundred languages" — a hundred ways of thinking, expressing, and understanding the world. Art, movement, storytelling, construction, music — these aren't extras. They are the learning.

Schools worldwide have been inspired by Reggio Emilia ever since, including Creativa Academy here in West Miami.

What Makes It Different from Traditional Preschool

In a traditional classroom, the teacher has a plan, the children follow it, and progress is measured by what children can recite back. In a Reggio-inspired classroom, things work differently.

The child is seen as capable. Rather than filling children with information, Reggio classrooms treat children as researchers. They notice, wonder, ask questions, and work things out — with teachers as guides rather than directors.

Projects emerge from children's interests. If the class becomes fascinated with shadows, that could become a week-long inquiry: What makes a shadow? Can we change its shape? Why does it disappear at noon? The teacher doesn't steer away from this — they lean in and deepen it.

The environment is a "third teacher." Reggio classrooms are intentionally beautiful and carefully arranged. Materials are real (not plastic toy substitutes). Natural light matters. Art and work made by children is displayed with care. The message this sends to a child is: your work matters. This space is made for you.

Creativity is woven into everything. Drawing, painting, building, storytelling — these aren't Friday afternoon activities. They're the primary way children explore and express ideas all week long.

What a Typical Day Looks Like at Creativa

When you drop your child off at Creativa Academy, they enter a prepared environment where they can dive into what interests them. The morning might begin with open exploration — a child building with blocks, another at the art table painting their view of a caterpillar, another working alongside a teacher on a letter-tracing tray.

Group time brings everyone together for a story, a song in Spanish and English, or a discussion about whatever theme the class is exploring that week. Then it's back to deeper work: art projects, sensory play, outdoor time, and guided activities connected to the weekly theme.

Teachers observe and document throughout the day. They take notes and photos, ask questions that extend thinking, and share observations with parents. This "documentation" is a core part of the Reggio approach — it makes children's thinking visible and helps families understand exactly how their child is growing.

What the Research Says

Beyond philosophy, there's strong evidence behind this approach. Research consistently shows that early childhood programs emphasizing play, inquiry, and social collaboration lead to better outcomes in creativity, problem-solving, and emotional regulation — skills that matter far more in the long run than being able to name the alphabet at age two.

Children who attend high-quality preschool programs like Creativa also tend to enter kindergarten with stronger self-regulation skills, which teachers consistently say is the strongest predictor of early school success.

Is Reggio Right for My Child?

The honest answer is that most children thrive in Reggio-inspired environments — especially children who are curious, social, or artistic. Children who need more structure can also do very well, because Reggio classrooms are consistent and predictable even while being flexible in content.

The best way to know is to come and see it. Children's reactions when they walk through the door tend to tell parents everything they need to know.
